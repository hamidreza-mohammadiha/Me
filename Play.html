Chance-based

Variable Reward

Definition: Mechanics that introduce randomness — prize draws, spin-the-wheel, scratch cards, mystery boxes. Driver: dopamine released in anticipation of an uncertain reward, plus the "near-miss" effect that keeps people trying just one more time.

🧠 Variable Ratio Reinforcement — B.F. Skinner, 1957 🎯 Octalysis Core Drive 7 — Unpredictability & Curiosity 🎲 Near-Miss Effect — Reid, 1986
Prize Draws / Raffles
Chance
Enter to win a prize after a qualifying action (purchase, demo, lead).
Example: Salesforce Dreamforce — attendees who visit 5+ booths enter a raffle for a MacBook.
Can attract non-serious participants; low perceived probability of winning.
Variable Ratio Reinforcement (Skinner, 1957) — of all reward schedules, an unpredictable payoff after a variable number of actions produces the highest, most persistent response rate and is the most resistant to extinction.
Spin-the-Wheel
Chance
Interactive wheel with segments offering discounts, prizes, or "try again."
Example: E-commerce exit-intent popup — spin for 10–50% off.
Can feel gimmicky; overuse dilutes brand value.
Near-Miss Effect (Reid, 1986) — a "Try Again" landing next to a big prize is processed by the brain almost like an actual win, fueling continued play.
Mystery Boxes
Chance
Customers pay a fixed price for a box with unknown value (surprise mechanics).
Example: Razer — "Mystery Box" with gaming gear worth up to 3× the price.
Regulatory scrutiny in some regions; can feel like gambling.
Information Gap Theory (Loewenstein, 1994) — curiosity is a felt deprivation; an unknown box creates a gap between what we know and want to know, which is intrinsically motivating to close.

Threshold Discounts

Loss Aversion · Urgency

Definition: Discounts unlocked at specific spending or volume thresholds. Driver: the goal-gradient effect (we accelerate as we approach a goal), loss aversion (missing the threshold feels like forfeiting a gain already "in hand"), and scarcity-driven urgency from countdowns.

⚖️ Loss Aversion / Prospect Theory — Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 🏁 Goal-Gradient Hypothesis — Hull, 1932; Kivetz et al., 2006 ⏳ Scarcity Principle — Cialdini, 1984
Fixed Discount at Target
Threshold
A set discount (e.g., 20%) is applied once the cart reaches a minimum value.
Example: "Spend $100, get 20% off" — common in retail and DTC.
Customers may add unneeded items to hit the threshold, increasing returns.
Loss Aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) — once a shopper is a few dollars short, not reaching the threshold is coded by the brain as losing the discount, not merely failing to gain it.
Scaling / Progressive Discounts
Threshold
Discount percentage increases stepwise with higher spend (e.g., 10% at $100, 15% at $200).
Example: B2B software — "Buy 10 licenses → 10% off; 25+ → 20% off."
Margin erosion at higher tiers; complexity in communication.
Goal-Gradient Hypothesis (Hull, 1932; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006) — motivation and effort intensify measurably as the distance to a reward shrinks, which is why the last tier feels most urgent.
Flash Countdown Discounts
Threshold
Time-limited discount with a visible countdown timer (e.g., "40% off — ends in 2h").
Example: Booking.com — "Last 2 rooms at this price!" with countdown.
Discount fatigue; customers may wait for the next flash sale.
Scarcity Principle (Cialdini, 1984) — opportunities appear more valuable as their availability decreases, especially when the constraint is time rather than quantity.

Bundling & Volume

Perceived Value · Anchoring

Definition: Incentives tied to purchasing multiple items or higher volume. Driver: anchoring on the bundle's combined "list price," and mental accounting that reframes several small purchases as one satisfying gain.

⚓ Anchoring Effect — Tversky & Kahneman, 1974 🧮 Mental Accounting — Richard Thaler, 1985 🎯 Decoy Effect — Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982
Bundling Discounts
Bundle
Multiple products sold together at a reduced price vs. buying separately.
Example: Microsoft 365 — bundled apps at a lower total than individual subscriptions.
Can cannibalize high-margin individual sales.
Mental Accounting (Thaler, 1985) — buyers evaluate the bundle as a single transaction, integrating the "losses" (price) and "gains" (multiple products), which reads as a better deal than the sum of separate purchases.
BOGO / Free 3rd-or-4th Product
Bundle
Buy one, get one free — or buy 3, get the 4th free (volume incentive).
Example: "Buy 2, get 3rd free" — common in CPG and retail.
Inventory strain; customers may stockpile and delay future purchases.
Anchoring Effect (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974) — "free" anchors attention disproportionately; a $0 item is valued far above its actual cost relative to a small percentage discount of equal monetary value.
Volume-Tier Pricing
Bundle
Per-unit price decreases as order quantity increases (wholesale-style).
Example: Alibaba — "1–99 units: $10/ea; 100–999: $8/ea; 1000+: $6/ea."
Smaller customers may feel penalized; complex to communicate.
Decoy Effect (Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982) — a visibly worse middle tier makes the top tier's per-unit value look like the obvious rational choice, nudging buyers toward higher volume.

Competitive / Ranking

Status · Competition

Definition: Mechanics that pit individuals or teams against each other. Driver: we evaluate our own performance by comparing it to others, and that comparison — not the prize alone — is often the stronger motivator.

👥 Social Comparison Theory — Leon Festinger, 1954 🎯 Octalysis Core Drive 5 — Social Influence & Relatedness 🧭 Self-Determination Theory — Competence, Deci & Ryan, 1985
Tiered Leagues & Knockouts
Competitive
Participants are grouped into leagues (e.g., Bronze → Diamond) with promotions/relegation.
Example: HubSpot's partner program — tiers based on revenue and certifications.
Demotivation for those stuck in lower tiers; sandbagging to stay in a winnable tier.
Social Comparison Theory (Festinger, 1954) — grouping peers of similar ability keeps comparisons "upward but attainable," which sustains motivation better than comparing everyone against the top performer.
Leaderboards
Competitive
Real-time ranking of performance (sales, points, etc.) with visibility to all.
Example: Salesforce's "Top Performer" dashboard visible to the whole sales org.
Can demotivate lower-ranked reps; may encourage short-term gaming.
Social Comparison Theory (Festinger, 1954) — visible rank triggers automatic upward comparison; effective for reps near the top, but can trigger disengagement for reps far from it without a segmented view.
1v1 Duels
Competitive
Two participants compete head-to-head in a timed challenge (e.g., most calls in 1 hour).
Example: Sales contests where two reps are paired and the winner gets a prize.
Can create unhealthy rivalry; losers may feel demoralized.
Competitive Arousal (Malhotra, 2010) — head-to-head framing raises physiological arousal beyond what the stakes alone justify, which is powerful but needs guardrails against escalation.
Team Quests
Competitive
Teams compete against each other to achieve a collective goal (e.g., revenue, demos).
Example: Regional sales teams competing for a "President's Club" trip.
Free-riding within teams; top performers may carry the load.
Self-Determination Theory — Relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 1985) — shared stakes satisfy the need to belong, which is why team-based competition often sustains effort longer than pure individual rivalry.

Progression / Loyalty

Achievement · Progress

Definition: Mechanics that reward ongoing engagement through points, badges, streaks, and tier progression. Driver: the feeling of being partway to a goal already ("endowed progress"), and the sense of mastery that satisfies the innate need for competence.

📈 Endowed Progress Effect — Nunes & Drèze, 2006 🧭 Self-Determination Theory — Competence & Autonomy 🔁 Operant Conditioning — B.F. Skinner, 1938
Points & Badges
Loyalty
Earn points for actions; redeem for rewards or unlock badges for milestones.
Example: Starbucks Rewards — stars earned per purchase, redeemable for free drinks.
Points inflation; diminishing perceived value over time.
Operant Conditioning (Skinner, 1938) — points act as an immediate, tangible reinforcement signal that strengthens the purchasing behavior that preceded it.
Streaks & Milestones
Loyalty
Reward consecutive actions (daily logins, weekly sales) or milestone achievements.
Example: Duolingo — daily streaks keep users coming back; sales teams with "100% quota" streaks.
Loss of streak can demotivate; may encourage "token" actions just to keep the streak alive.
Loss Aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) applied to streaks — once a streak exists, it's mentally "owned," so breaking it is coded as a loss, which is a stronger pull than the original reward for starting it.
Tier Progression (Bronze → Silver → Gold)
Loyalty
Users advance through tiers based on cumulative activity, unlocking better perks.
Example: American Express — Green → Gold → Platinum with increasing benefits.
Customers may "churn" after reaching the top tier; sandbagging near tier cutoffs.
Endowed Progress Effect (Nunes & Drèze, 2006) — people who feel they've already made headway toward a goal (even artificially) complete it faster; a visible "60% to Gold" bar exploits exactly this effect.

Social

Social Proof · Belonging

Definition: Mechanics that leverage social connections and networks. Driver: the obligation to reciprocate a gift, the pull of watching others act first, and the need to belong to a community.

🤝 Reciprocity Principle — Cialdini, 1984 👀 Social Proof — Cialdini, 1984 🧭 Self-Determination Theory — Relatedness
Referral Gamification
Social
Reward users for referring others, often with a points or tier system for referrals.
Example: Uber — "Give $20, get $20" referral credits; Dropbox's extra storage for referrals.
Can attract low-quality referrals; gaming through fake accounts.
Reciprocity Principle (Cialdini, 1984) — giving the referred friend a credit first creates a felt obligation on both sides of the exchange, which raises acceptance rates versus a one-sided referral bonus.
Team-Based Challenges
Social
Groups work together toward a shared goal, with rewards split among the team.
Example: Strava — club challenges where members log miles together; sales teams with group quotas.
Free-riding; high performers may feel they carry the team.
Social Proof (Cialdini, 1984) — seeing teammates actively participating signals "this is the correct behavior here," which pulls in members who would otherwise sit out.

The Frameworks Underneath

Theory

Every mechanic in this playbook maps back to one of four foundational models from behavioral psychology, motivation science, and behavioral economics. Knowing which lever you're pulling — and why it works on the brain — is what separates durable gamification from a gimmick that wears off in a month.

Octalysis Framework
Design Model
Developed by Yu-kai Chou in Actionable Gamification (2015). Maps every gamified experience to 8 "Core Drives": Epic Meaning, Development & Accomplishment, Empowerment of Creativity, Ownership, Social Influence & Relatedness, Scarcity & Impatience, Unpredictability & Curiosity, and Avoidance.
Use it to: audit a mechanic and ask "which Core Drive is this actually triggering, and is it a healthy 'White Hat' drive (progress, purpose) or an urgent 'Black Hat' one (scarcity, fear of loss)?"
Reference: Chou, Y. (2015). Actionable Gamification: Beyond Points, Badges, and Leaderboards.
Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
Motivation Science
Developed by psychologists Edward Deci & Richard Ryan (1985). Argues intrinsic motivation depends on satisfying three innate needs: Autonomy (choice), Competence (mastery/progress), and Relatedness (belonging).
Use it to: explain why leaderboards and tier systems keep working long after the novelty fades — they feed Competence and Relatedness, not just a cash prize.
Reference: Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (1985). Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior.
Fogg Behavior Model
Behavior Design
Developed by BJ Fogg (Stanford, 2009): B = MAP. A Behavior happens only when sufficient Motivation, Ability (low friction), and a well-timed Prompt converge at the same moment.
Use it to: diagnose why a mechanic isn't working — is it a motivation problem, a friction/ability problem, or simply a missing trigger/reminder?
Reference: Fogg, B. J. (2009). "A Behavior Model for Persuasive Design." Persuasive '09 Proceedings.
Prospect Theory & Principles of Influence
Behavioral Economics
Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky's Prospect Theory (1979) shows losses loom roughly twice as large as equivalent gains (loss aversion). Robert Cialdini's six principles of influence (1984) — reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity — explain why specific offer framing works.
Use it to: understand why "don't lose your streak" outperforms "earn a bonus," and why countdown timers convert better than plain discounts.
Reference: Kahneman & Tversky (1979), Econometrica; Cialdini, R. (1984). Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion.
